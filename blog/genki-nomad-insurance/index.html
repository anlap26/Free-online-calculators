Genki and the cover a move actually needs

Travel insurance, nomad medical cover, and local health insurance are three contracts. This page says which lines to read, and quotes no premium.

Medical cover for a person who moves around is three different products that share a vocabulary and not much else. Travel insurance is built around a trip. It often starts from a home country, often pays for an emergency rather than a Tuesday appointment, and often ends when the trip ends. Nomad or expat medical insurance is built around a person who is outside a home system for longer. It may be a subscription, it may still exclude the ordinary doctor, and it is still a contract with a schedule of benefits. Local health insurance is the scheme of the country where you actually live. A residence permit sometimes demands it. A policy bought from abroad sometimes does not satisfy the immigration office. Those three questions have to be answered separately, in that order: what trip are you on, which system are you outside, and which permit are you trying to keep.

Genki is the name this site uses when people are shopping in the second category, and sometimes at the edge of the first. This page says what to read. It does not quote a premium, a payout rate, or a medical outcome. Those change with age, passport, deductible, and the wording of a condition you already have. The number on a comparison site is not the number on the schedule you will be sent.

How to read a policy without a lab

You do not need a claims database to compare two policies. You need the table of benefits, the exclusions, the definition of pre-existing, the definition of home country, the age rule, the deductible, the sports wording, and the evacuation wording. Put the two tables side by side and mark each line as paid, capped, or absent. A marketing page that says comprehensive is not a line in the table. If a benefit is missing from the table, assume it is missing from the contract until a person at the insurer writes otherwise. Plans change. Product names change. Read the PDF that belongs to the application in front of you, not a review that paraphrased last year's PDF.

Genki is a German provider known among nomads. People talk about Explorer-style and resident-style products, and about cover that looks more like a membership you keep while you move than a policy bought for one set of flight dates. Those names change, and a name in a forum thread is not the contract. Do not buy from the reputation of the name. Open the current plan, read who the underwriter is, and read the eligibility questions. Your passport and the country you live in now are usually on that form. A subscription you can pause is a different contract from one that renews only if you are outside your home country. The page will say which you are looking at. Believe the page. The brand on the website and the insurer named on the contract can be different companies. Write down the insurer. That is who the claim goes to, whatever the logo on the advert.

The useful checks are the ones that hurt later. Does a routine visit exist as a benefit or only an emergency. Is evacuation to the nearest suitable hospital the same benefit as a flight to your home city. What does the policy call a pre-existing condition: a diagnosis, a prescription, a symptom, a look-back period. Is there a deductible per event or per period. None of those answers is stable enough to copy into this article. They are stable enough that you can find them in an afternoon.

A German provider does not mean the policy behaves like German statutory insurance, and it does not mean a residence permit in Germany or anywhere else will accept it. Permits ask for what they ask for. Bring the schedule to that question, or ask the authority which document they want. Genki's place in a shortlist is that nomads already discuss it, so the help pages and the policy are findable. Findable is not the same as suitable. Suitable is the table of benefits matched to the care you actually use.

Emergency, routine care, and conditions you already have

Emergency cover is for an accident or a sudden illness that needs treatment now. Routine cover is the general practitioner, the repeat prescription, the dental check, the maternity care, the mental-health visit. Many travel policies are emergency-only. Some nomad medical products add a slice of routine care and some do not. Read the line, including the cap and the waiting period. A waiting period means the benefit is not there on day one even if it is printed on the brochure.

Pre-existing conditions are the argument that decides real claims, and this page will not predict yours. Policies define the term differently. One looks at anything diagnosed or treated in a period of years. Another looks at symptoms you had even without a diagnosis. A third offers a moratorium: the condition is excluded until you have gone a stated time without treatment or advice. A fourth excludes it forever. Genki's wording can differ by product. Read the definition that applies to the application you submit. Do not assume either brand covers a condition because a friend with a different condition was paid.

Home country, age, and the deductible

Home-country cover is where long-stay policies surprise people. Many limit or exclude care in the country of citizenship or permanent residence, or they cover a short visit and then stop. If you go back for a month to see family, the month can be the month you are uninsured for anything except a suitcase. Read the clause before you book the ticket. A policy that is excellent everywhere except the place you still call home is a specific product. It can still be the right product. It is the wrong product to discover at the clinic door.

Age changes eligibility and price. Some products refuse new members past an age, some change benefits, some only change the premium. This article will not quote the cutoff or the premium. Check the current age rule for the product, not for the brand in the abstract. The deductible is the amount you pay before the policy pays. A higher deductible is often sold as a lower premium. Confirm that relationship on the quote you were given, and read whether a separate coinsurance or an annual cap sits behind it. A deductible you cannot pay is not a saving.

Sports and evacuation

Sports exclusions are narrower than the word adventure. A policy may cover a gym and exclude skiing, diving, climbing, or anything with an engine, or it may sell those as an add-on. The activity you actually do is the one to search for in the exclusions. If it is not named, ask, and keep the answer. Evacuation is several benefits wearing one word. Medical evacuation to a suitable facility, repatriation to a home country, and evacuation because of a political crisis are different promises. A policy can include one. Who decides that a flight is medically necessary is part of the promise. Read that sentence. A large number on a marketing page is not the sentence.

What the day count and the cost index are not

The stay calculator has a year mode that counts distinct days in a calendar year. Many countries publish a 183-day discussion in tax residency. That count is not an insurance trigger. Insurers use their own definitions of trip length, residence, and home country. Being under 183 days does not start a policy. Being over 183 days does not stop one, and it does not tell a border how long you may stay. The Schengen mode is a 90-in-180 day count for trips you type in. Who it fits

A short holiday with a return ticket and a home system waiting is often a travel-insurance question. A nomad subscription can be the wrong shape even if the advert speaks to travellers. Someone who will be outside any national system for months should read a nomad or international medical policy, and should ask whether a residence permit will demand a local policy on top. Genki belongs on that reading list because nomads already use it as the name of a German provider with Explorer-style and resident-style products. It does not belong on the list as a winner. The schedule of benefits decides, together with eligibility for your passport.

Questions people ask

Is nomad insurance the same as travel insurance?

No. Travel insurance is built around a trip, often from a home country, and often pays for an emergency rather than a routine appointment. Nomad or expat medical cover is built around a longer stay outside a home system and may be sold as a subscription. Either can still exclude the ordinary doctor. Local health insurance is a third product, and a residence permit may demand it specifically. Read the schedule of the policy you were offered. A marketing sentence that says comprehensive is not a line in that schedule.
