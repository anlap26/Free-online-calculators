Cloudways and Kinsta, and the files that still decide speed

Two managed hosts compared by what you still administer and what neither of them writes. No time-to-first-byte number is invented here.

Managed hosting is the product you buy when you want someone else to run the server layer and you still want a site that is yours: your pages, your theme, your content. It is not a designer, and it is not a faster theme. Cloudways and Kinsta are two names people put on a shortlist for that product, and they arrived there by different routes. This page says how to tell the routes apart and what still dominates the speed of a page after you have picked a host. It does not publish a time to first byte. We did not measure one.

A host answers questions a page cannot answer for itself. Who patches the system. Who restarts a process when it dies. Where backups go and how you restore one. Whether a staging copy exists so a plugin update is not tested on the live site. Those are real jobs. They are also jobs you can confirm on a current plan page in an afternoon. A benchmark blog that crowns a brand from one afternoon in one city is a different kind of document. This article will not add another crown.

Cloudways

Cloudways has been a layer on top of cloud providers the customer picks. You choose a provider and a size. The managed layer has covered the sort of work a person does not want to do by hand: the stack, updates, backups as a feature you still confirm, and a panel in front of a machine that remains a cloud provider's machine. The list of providers changes. A name you remember from a tutorial may be gone, renamed, or priced differently. Read the current list. Do not inherit it from this paragraph.

Because you pick the underlying provider, you also inherit a reading assignment. Which region will the machine sit in. What does the size mean for disk and memory. What is billed by the managed layer and what, if anything, is billed by the cloud provider on top. Those answers are on the current pricing page, and they move. This article will not quote them. A managed layer of this kind is not the same product as a host that only offers a finished application. You may still be closer to a server than on a platform that never shows you one. Confirm which parts you still administer. If the answer is "more than I wanted", that is a useful answer, caught before the invoice.

Kinsta

Kinsta has built its name on managed WordPress hosting. People look it up when the site is WordPress and they want the host to own the stack, the caching approach, and the staging copy, with limits written as visits, storage, and features on a plan. Confirm those limits on the current page. A memory of what a plan included is not a plan. If you need a shop-front that is not WordPress, do not assume this description still matches the product. Kinsta has offered more than WordPress at times. Read the current product list and buy the product that matches the software you will actually run.

Managed WordPress does not write your theme, does not choose your plugins, and does not make a heavy page light. It can provide a staging site, backups, and a person to ask when the stack itself is the problem. Whether those are included, and at what limit, is the plan. A site that is mostly a store, or mostly an application that is not WordPress, may be on the wrong page of the catalogue even when the brand is the right one to ask. Read the page. The brand is not the page.

What neither host writes

Neither company writes your theme. A theme that loads a dozen fonts, a chat widget on every template, and a full-size photograph in the first screen will be slow on a careful host and slow on a careless one. The host brand is the smaller term in that sum more often than the advertising suggests. The work that moves the number is usually in the files you control.

Start with the image. A huge image dominates transfer size. The image compressor on this site redraws the bitmap in the browser and never uploads it. You set a maximum width and a quality, then you download the smaller file and put that file on the site. The host does not need to be involved, and the tool does not know which host you use. Do this before you compare hosts. Otherwise you will compare two companies at serving a file you should not have shipped.

A chat widget is a second site inside the first. It calls other servers, it waits, and it shifts layout. Remove it on a staging copy and look at the page. If the page becomes obviously quicker, the widget was the finding. A font file can block the first text. One family, with the weights you use, is a decision a host does not make. The pixel converter is a reminder that layout math is yours: it turns pixels into rem from a root size you type. It is not a hosting feature. The contrast checker tells you whether text and background meet a contrast bar you can read about on the tool page. Accessibility is not included in a server plan, and a fast server does not fix a pale grey paragraph.

The revenue model, and the 7 percent

The page-speed revenue calculator is a model, not a measurement of Cloudways or of Kinsta. You type visits, the conversion you have now, revenue per conversion, and how much of that conversion you assume each extra second removes. The form starts with 7 percent per extra second. That 7 percent is an assumption, a placeholder so the form is not empty. It is not a law, not a study this site ran, and not a property of either host. Replace it with a figure you are willing to defend, or with zero if you refuse to pretend. The result is what your assumption implies for a month and for a year. It is not a time to first byte, and this article has no time to first byte to offer.

Use the model after you have a reason to believe seconds are on the table: a large image you have not compressed, a widget you have not tried removing, a font you have not counted. Buying a more expensive host while leaving those in place spends the money on the smaller term. The Semrush guide is the matching point about demand. A faster response does not create a query nobody types. Speed and research are different levers. Pull the one the page actually lacks.

Questions the plan page can answer

Read backups, staging, and the software versions as questions, not as a checklist this page fills in. How many days of backups, and do you restore them yourself. Is staging on the plan you can afford, or on a higher one. Which versions of the language and of the database are offered, and what happens when you need one they do not offer. Is email included. Many managed WordPress hosts are not your mailbox. A site that assumes the host will receive mail for the domain can discover the gap on the day a password reset goes nowhere. Check. A content-delivery feature, if the plan mentions one, has its own limits and its own cache rules. Read them before you blame the origin for a stale page.

Migration is a separate line. Some plans include help moving a site, some charge, some leave it to you. A trial, if one exists, is the moment to learn the panel, not a promise that the trial price continues. Confirm the current trial, the current renewal, and what happens to the site if you leave. Write those three down. They matter more than a slogan about speed.

Cache, and a page that is slow for a logged-in user

A cached page served to a stranger can look quick while the same page, for a logged-in editor, waits on the application. When you compare hosts, say which of those two you mean. A marketing screenshot is almost always the stranger's view. Your authors live in the other view. Ask, on the current help page, what the cache does with logged-in sessions, with a cart, and with a page that must be different for each visitor. A host that is excellent at the public cache can still leave the editor waiting. That is not a contradiction. It is two products sharing a brand.

Also ask what you can flush yourself. A stale cache after a design change is a support ticket only if the panel will not let you clear it. The image you compressed does nothing until the old file is gone from the cache. The contrast fix does nothing until the stylesheet is the stylesheet you edited. These are ordinary publishing steps. They are easy to blame on the host brand because the brand is the thing you just paid. Check the panel before you write the complaint.

Who it fits

Cloudways fits someone who wants to choose the underlying cloud provider, accepts that the list of providers changes, and is willing to read which parts they still administer. It is a weak fit if you wanted no server-shaped decisions at all. Kinsta fits someone whose site is WordPress and who will confirm visits, storage, staging, and backups on the current plan rather than from memory. It is a weak fit if the thing you need to host is not WordPress and you have not checked that the current catalogue has a product for it. Neither fits someone who needed a person to design the theme. A static site may need neither. This page does not survey static hosts. It says the application you run comes before the brand.
