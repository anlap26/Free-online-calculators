The meeting planner takes a date, a clock time, and the IANA zone that time belongs to, then converts that instant into the other zones you tick. The zone of the typed time is always shown. Conversion uses IANA zones through the browser's Intl data, not a fixed offset such as a permanent plus zero or minus five. Daylight saving is handled for the date you pick. A summer date and a winter date can disagree for the same cities, because the zone data supplies the offset civil time uses on that date.

A conversion that is true on one date

Here is a pair you can check. 15:00 in Europe/London on 19 November 2026 is 15:00 UTC, and the same instant is 10:00 in America/New_York. On that day the UK is on GMT, so London local time and UTC match. New York is on EST, five hours behind UTC. The five-hour gap is a fact about 19 November 2026. It is not a rule you should paste into every later meeting. On a date when one city has changed its clock and the other has not, a spreadsheet that still says minus five will be wrong. Pick the date of the call and let the page recompute. The names in the form are IANA names, such as Europe/London and America/New_York, not a homemade abbreviation.

Each row is marked according to whether that local hour sits inside 09:00 to 17:00. An hour is marked inside when it is at least 09:00 and earlier than 17:00. 17:00 itself is outside the mark. The window is a convention, not a law. A team that works a late overlap, a split shift, or a four-day week is not doing anything this page can forbid. The mark is a highlight so you can see who is inside a common office span and who is at breakfast or at the end of the day. Move the clock time if the highlight misses the people who have to speak.

Where the UTC hours come from

Besides the instant you typed, the page walks the UTC hours of that calendar date. For each hour it counts how many selected cities have a local hour inside 09:00 to 17:00, then lists the UTC hours where the most selected cities fall inside the window together. When several hours tie, a short list is shown. The list is an overlap hint. It does not know public holidays or a person who is travelling. A city left ticked still counts in the overlap, so untick anyone who will not be on the call.

  • Type the time in the zone it belongs to. The page will not assume the time is UTC unless you pick UTC.
  • Tick only the cities that will be on the call. The home zone is included either way.
  • Read 09:00 as inside and 17:00 as outside. The span is a convention.
  • Change the date if the call is not on the day currently in the form. Offsets belong to the date.
  • Use the overlap list as a hint, then confirm with the people who have to attend.

Questions people ask

Does the planner handle daylight saving?

Yes. It uses IANA zones through the browser's Intl data, not a fixed offset. The offset is the one for the date you pick, including a day next to a clock change. A summer call and a winter call between the same cities can differ. A spreadsheet that keeps a permanent minus five is wrong on a day when only one place has moved. Put the date of the call in the form and read that result.

Why is 15:00 in London also 10:00 in New York on 19 November 2026?

On 19 November 2026 the UK is on GMT, so 15:00 in Europe/London is 15:00 UTC. New York is on EST that day, five hours behind UTC, so the same instant is 10:00 in America/New_York. The pair is true for that date. It is not a gap to copy onto every other day. When one city changes its clock and the other does not, the gap changes. Pick the date and let the zone data supply the offset.

Is 09:00 to 17:00 a legal working day?

No. It is a convention used as a highlight. A local hour is inside when it is at least 09:00 and earlier than 17:00, so 17:00 itself is outside. A team can work earlier, later, or on another pattern. The page has no rule it can enforce. The mark shows who is inside a common office span. If the people who must speak are outside it, move the time instead of treating the highlight as a law.

What are the UTC hours the page lists?

For the date you picked, the page checks each UTC hour and counts how many selected cities are inside 09:00 to 17:00. It lists the UTC hours where the most selected cities fall inside that window together. A tie is shown as a short list. A city you left ticked still counts, even if that person will miss the call. The list is an overlap hint. It does not know holidays or a week when someone is travelling.
