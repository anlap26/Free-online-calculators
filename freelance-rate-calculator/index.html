The freelance rate calculator turns a net income you want to keep into a gross amount you would have to bill, then divides that gross by the hours you can bill. Gross equals net income plus yearly business costs, divided by one minus the tax rate. The tax rate is the percent you type. It is not a bracket the page looks up, and it is not a tax return. A rate of 100 percent or more is rejected, because the divisor would be zero or negative. Business costs are the yearly figure you type. The page will not invent them.

Work days and billable hours

Work days equal 52 minus the vacation weeks, multiplied by 5, then minus sick days, then minus weekday public holidays. A vacation week comes out before the multiply, so one vacation week removes five days. Sick days and weekday holidays come off as single days. The model is 52 weeks, not the real weekday count of a named year. If the result is zero or below, the page stops rather than printing a rate. Billable hours equal those work days, times the hours you could work in a week divided by 5, times utilization.

Utilization is the share of working time a client pays for. Meetings you cannot bill, admin, and unpaid marketing are not billable. At 100 percent every working hour is treated as billed. A lower share means the same gross is earned in fewer hours, so the hourly figure rises. The percent is yours. The day rate is the hourly rate times the hours in a working day, and it is not cut by utilization a second time. The monthly figure is the gross divided by 12.

Two shapes of the same formula

First shape, kept simple so the hours are easy to see. Net income 52000, tax rate 0, business costs 0, no vacation weeks, no sick days, no weekday holidays, 40 hours in a week, utilization 100 percent. Gross equals 52000. Work days equal 52 times 5, which is 260. A working day is 40 divided by 5, which is 8 hours. Billable hours equal 260 times 8, which is 2080. The hourly rate is 52000 divided by 2080, which is 25 per hour. The day rate is 25 times 8, which is 200.

Second shape, closer to a year with tax and costs. Tax 25 percent, business costs 5000, net income 60000. The sum inside is 60000 plus 5000, which is 65000. One minus 0.25 is 0.75. Gross equals 65000 divided by 0.75. That division is 86666.67 when it is shown to the cent. Vacation, sick days, weekday holidays, the weekly hours, and utilization still sit on top of this gross. They do not change 65000 divided by 0.75. They change how many billable hours carry that gross, and therefore the hourly rate. The 260-day shape is a ceiling of this model, not a promise that every weekday is worked.

Questions people ask

How is the gross figure calculated?

Gross equals the net you want plus yearly business costs, divided by one minus the tax rate you type. At a tax rate of zero and costs of zero, gross equals the net. With net 60000, costs 5000, and tax of 25 percent, gross is 65000 divided by 0.75, or 86666.67 to the cent. The rate is a percent you chose. The page does not look up a bracket, and 100 percent or more is rejected.

Where do 260 work days and 25 per hour come from?

They come from an empty year: net 52000, tax 0, costs 0, no leave, 40 hours a week, and 100 percent utilization. Work days are 52 times 5, which is 260. The working day is 8 hours, so billable hours are 260 times 8, which is 2080. Divide 52000 by 2080 and the hour is 25. The day rate is 25 times 8, which is 200. The model uses 52 weeks, not the exact weekdays of one calendar year.

What does utilization mean?

It is the share of working time a client pays for. Meetings you cannot bill, admin, and unpaid marketing are not billable. At 100 percent every working hour carries the gross. A lower share means fewer hours carry the same gross, so the hourly rate rises. The day rate is that hourly rate times a full working day. Do not apply utilization a second time. The percent is yours. The page does not know your week.

Is this a tax return?

No. It is arithmetic on a tax percent you typed. It does not know your brackets, which contributions are deductible, or which costs a tax office would accept. Business costs are included only because you typed them. The monthly figure is the gross divided by 12, an even split, not a filing calendar. Keep the output as a planning rate. A return belongs to the rules that cover you.

Does the hourly rate include a Wise fee or a medical premium?

No. A cross-border payout has its own fee, separate from the hourly rate. Wise is the payout name on this page, and today's fee is not stored here. A solo year with no employer sick pay is why people look at nomad medical plans. Genki is the medical name in the guide, which says what to read in that plan. Neither a premium nor a payout fee sits inside the 25 per hour.
