Simple interest calculator

Interest that does not compound: principal times the annual rate times the time, with months and days turned into years first.

Simple interest is not compound interest. A rate that is credited and then earns on itself belongs on the compound page.

The formula is I = P × r × t. P is the principal. r is the annual rate as a decimal, so 5 percent is 0.05. t is the time in years. The amount at the end is P + I. Nothing is added back onto the principal along the way, which is the whole difference from compound interest. If you type a monthly rate into the annual box, the result is twelve times too big. The label on the rate is annual on purpose.

Time in years is used as typed. Time in months is divided by 12, so 6 months is half a year and 18 months is 1.5 years. Time in days is divided by 365 or by 360, whichever base you pick. The 365 base is an actual/365 count: each day is 1/365 of the rate, including in a leap year. The 360 base divides by 360, which is the style of a 30/360 fraction when someone has already turned a period into a day count of 30-day months. The page does not itself convert a pair of dates into a 30/360 day count. If you have two dates, count the days you intend, or use the date calculator, and then type that count here.

Three results you can recompute

Start with 1,000 at 5 percent for 3 years. Interest is 1,000 × 0.05 × 3, which is 150. The amount at the end is 1,150. The same principal and rate for 6 months uses t = 6/12 = 0.5, so the interest is 25 and the amount is 1,025. For 73 days on a 365 base, t = 73/365, and the interest is 1,000 × 0.05 × 73/365, about 10.00 only if the day count were 73 on a base that makes it round. It is not forced to a round number. For 72 days on a 360 base, t = 72/360 = 0.2, and the interest is exactly 10. Those four cases are the checks the formula is held to.

A zero rate returns zero interest and an ending amount equal to the principal, for any time. A zero time does the same. A negative principal is rejected. The page does not subtract tax, and it does not inflate the result. If the money will sit long enough that interest is credited and then earns interest, this page understates the balance on purpose. That question is compound interest, including the choice of how often the rate compounds and whether a monthly deposit arrives at the start or the end of the month.

Where simple interest is the wrong tool

  • A savings balance that compounds monthly is not I = P × r × t.
  • A mortgage payment is an amortizing payment, not this interest added once at the end.
  • The day base does not know which calendar convention a bank wrote into a note.
  • A rate quoted as a monthly fee has to be turned into an annual rate before you type it, or the label is wrong.

Compound growth with deposits is the compound interest calculator. A payment that pays the loan down is the loan calculator or the mortgage calculator. Purchasing power of the ending amount is the inflation calculator. Count calendar days between two dates on the date calculator before you decide which day count to type here.

Questions people ask

What is the simple interest formula?

Interest equals principal times the annual rate times the time in years. The amount at the end is the principal plus that interest. The interest is not added back onto the principal.

How are months and days turned into years?

Months are divided by 12. Days are divided by 365 or by 360, depending on the base you pick. The 365 base does not give February 29 a special weight.

Is 5 percent for 3 years on 1,000 equal to 150?

Yes. 1,000 times 0.05 times 3 is 150, and the ending amount is 1,150. Six months at the same rate is 25 of interest.

When should I use compound interest instead?

When the interest is credited and then itself earns interest, or when you add a deposit every month. Simple interest leaves both of those out on purpose.
