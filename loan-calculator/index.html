Loan calculator

The payment on a fixed-rate loan, the interest over the term, and the effect of paying a little extra each month.

This is loan arithmetic for a rate and a term you type. It is not a credit offer.

The term is the years you type, times twelve, plus the extra months. The monthly rate is the annual rate divided by twelve, the same convention as a fixed installment loan. The contractual payment is the amortizing payment: principal times the monthly rate times one plus that rate, raised to the number of months, divided by that power minus one. A zero rate makes the payment the principal divided by the number of months. An extra amount you type is added to that contractual payment in a month-by-month balance. Each month interest is the remaining balance times the monthly rate. The payment cannot be smaller than that interest, or the balance would grow and the page stops. The last month pays only what is left.

Total interest is the sum of those monthly interest amounts, not contractual payment times months minus principal, because the last payment and any extra payment change the cash that actually leaves. Interest saved is the interest on the contractual schedule minus the interest on the schedule with the extra payment. If the extra box is zero, the two schedules are the same and the saving is zero. Months with the extra payment is how many months the balance takes to reach zero. It cannot be longer than the contractual term when the extra payment is positive and the rate is not negative.

Two loans you can recompute

Borrow 20,000 at 7.5 percent for 5 years and pay nothing extra. The term is 60 months. The monthly rate is 0.075 divided by 12. The payment line is the amortizing formula on those inputs. Total interest is what the schedule charges above the 20,000. Add 100 a month on top of the contractual payment and the payoff month count falls, the interest falls, and the saving line is the difference. The page does not assume a fee for paying early. If your contract charges one, it is not in this result.

A zero-rate check is exact. Borrow 1,200 at 0 percent for 12 months. The payment is 100. Pay an extra 100 and the loan is gone in 6 months, the cash paid is still 1,200, and the interest saved is zero because there was no interest. That case catches a formula that accidentally compounds a zero rate or that forgets the last partial payment. A term of zero months is rejected. A negative amount is rejected. The same formula covers a car loan or a personal loan when the rate is fixed and the payment is monthly. It does not cover a credit card that recalculates a minimum from a changing balance, and it does not cover interest-only periods.

What stays outside

  • One rate covers the whole term. A promotional rate that steps up needs two runs.
  • Origination fees are not added to the principal unless you type them into the amount borrowed.
  • A penalty for paying early is not subtracted from the interest saved.
  • Weekly or biweekly schedules are not built. The step is one month.
  • The result is not a disclosure a lender is required to give you.

A home price, a down payment, and yearly tax and insurance belong on the mortgage calculator. Interest with no payments along the way is the simple interest calculator. Money you set aside so that it grows is the compound interest calculator. The gap between a cost and a price, which is a different percentage from a loan rate, is the margin and markup calculator.

Questions people ask

How is the loan payment calculated?

Monthly rate equals the annual rate divided by 12. Payment equals principal times that rate times (1 + rate) to the power of the number of months, divided by that power minus 1. A zero rate divides the principal by the number of months.

What does the extra payment do?

It is added to the contractual payment every month. Interest is still charged on the remaining balance. The balance reaches zero sooner, and the interest saved is the difference between the two schedules.

Why can total paid differ from payment times months?

The last month pays only the remaining balance plus that month's interest. An extra payment also changes how much cash leaves. The total-paid line sums the payments that were actually applied.

Does this work for a credit card?

No. A card minimum usually changes as the balance changes, and the rate can change. This page wants one fixed rate and one fixed monthly step.
