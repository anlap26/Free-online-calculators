Password generator

A random password or a passphrase, drawn in this browser, with the entropy printed beside it.

Passwords, photos, and text stay in this browser, except the IP test, which says which public service it calls.

This page builds a password or a passphrase on your device and shows the entropy next to it. The characters come from crypto.getRandomValues, not from Math.random. Math.random is a general-purpose generator, and it is the wrong tool for a secret. Draws that would make some characters more likely than others are thrown away, so the alphabet stays even. Nothing is uploaded. There is no account. When you close the tab, this page no longer holds the string.

How the bits are counted

Entropy for a random password is the length times log2 of the alphabet size. A 12-character password from 62 symbols, a to z, A to Z, and 0 to 9, is about 71.5 bits. That is 12 times log2 of 62, and it describes the space only when every string is equally likely. The time next to a result is a 50 percent chance under a random search of that whole space. It is not a date until which the password is guaranteed to hold. At 10 billion guesses a second, the fast offline rate this page uses, that chance for 71.5 bits arrives on the order of 5,100 years. The slow figure is 10 thousand guesses a second, closer to an online form that slows people down. Both assume the attacker already has something to check, such as a stolen hash, and tries the whole space in random order.

The form does not open on the 12-character example. It starts at 16 characters, with lower case, upper case, and digits on, symbols off, and lookalikes such as 0, O, 1, and l removed. Letters and digits with those lookalikes removed are 57 symbols, not 62. A longer string from that slightly smaller set still carries more entropy than the worked example, about 93 bits at the default length, but the number to trust is the one printed beside the password you actually generated. Turning symbols on enlarges the alphabet. A separator you choose between passphrase words is fixed by you, so it adds no bits.

Why 48 real bits can beat a clever password

Human passwords fall to dictionaries much faster than the alphabet formula suggests. People capitalise a word, swap in a digit, and append a year. Attackers try leaked lists and those patterns before they ever walk from a to z. A shorter password that looks clever, because it has a capital and a symbol, can still be one entry on that list. Length times log2 of a huge alphabet then overstates the work, because that alphabet is not the search they run.

Passphrase entropy is the word count times log2 of the list size. This site's list has 256 words, so each word is 8 bits, because 2 to the 8 is 256. Six words are 48 bits. The Russian list is another 256 words and the same arithmetic. The words are picked uniformly, with the biased draws thrown away. Forty-eight bits of a real passphrase can beat a shorter clever password that is already in a dictionary: the dictionary search ends quickly, and the random words are still work the attacker has to do. That holds only if you keep the words the page dealt. A pet name or a proverb spends the bits. At 10 billion guesses a second, a 50 percent chance against 48 bits is about four hours, not 5,100 years. At 10 thousand guesses a second it is about 450 years. Each extra word adds 8 bits. Four words from this list are only 32 bits, and at the fast rate a 50 percent chance is about a fifth of a second. Six is the default on the form for a reason.

Store a unique password

A strong password you then reuse is already lost if one site leaks it. No entropy figure on this page can see that leak. Keep a separate password for each account in a manager. Proton and Bitwarden both store those entries. Plans change, so read the current offer rather than a price copied from an article. The password manager guide describes what to compare. Use the strength checker when you want the penalties for repeats and sequences applied to something you typed yourself. A key for the AES encryptor belongs in the same manager. A hash is a fingerprint, not a login. The address builder makes a string and does not open a mailbox.

Questions people ask

Is the password sent to a server?

No. crypto.getRandomValues runs in your browser, and the string is shown on the page. This site never receives it. Closing the tab drops it from the page.

Why does my result not say 71.5 bits?

71.5 bits is the worked example of 12 characters from all 62 letters and digits. The form starts at 16 characters and, unless you keep lookalikes, uses 57 symbols. Read the entropy printed next to the password you generated.

Is a six-word passphrase stronger than every shorter password?

It beats a short password that is really a dictionary entry. Six random words from this 256-word list are 48 bits. Against a fast offline attacker who knows the list, a 50 percent chance is about four hours. Against human habits, the random words usually win.

Why not Math.random?

Math.random is not built to be unpredictable. This page uses crypto.getRandomValues and discards draws that would make some characters more likely than others.

Should I reuse this password on a second site?

Reuse means one leak opens the other account. Store a unique password in a manager. Proton and Bitwarden are two options here, and their plans change.
