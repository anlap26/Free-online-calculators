The independence number is annual spending divided by the withdrawal rate. Spending of 40000 and a withdrawal rate of 0.04 give 1,000,000. At 4 percent the portfolio has to be 25 times one year of spending. That 4 percent figure is a rule of thumb from a historical research tradition about long retirements and mixed portfolios. It is not a guarantee that a future sequence of returns will support the same spending. A higher withdrawal rate makes the target smaller and the plan stricter. A lower rate makes the target larger. The spending you type is the amount you would take out, before tax. The form does not know your brackets.

The gap and the dividend line

The gap is the independence number minus the portfolio, and it is not shown below zero. A portfolio of 250000 against 1,000,000 leaves a gap of 750,000. If the portfolio is already at or above the number, the gap is zero and the time is zero. Dividend income is the portfolio times the yield you type, so 250000 at 3 percent is 7500 a year. That cash is not the withdrawal rate. Four percent of 1,000,000 is 40000, which is the spending you typed, and 7500 does not cover 40000. The dividend line is not subtracted from the gap. Treating it as the plan double-counts a yield the withdrawal rate may already be assuming.

Years to the number, and the coast sum

Time to the number uses the same monthly compounding model as the compound interest calculator, with one difference you should not miss. Compounding is fixed at 12 times a year, so the monthly rate is the nominal return divided by 12, and contributions are treated as end-of-month. The page solves that future-value equation for the number of months until the balance reaches the independence number. With a 5 percent nominal return compounded monthly and no new contribution, 250000 grows to 1,000,000 in about 27.8 years. That is the formula under a constant rate, not a forecast. If contributions are zero and the return is zero, a gap is never closed. The page says the contributions and the return do not reach the number.

Coast FIRE is the sum that grows to the independence number with no new contributions across the age gap you type. The age gap is the age you would stop adding money, minus your age now. The coast sum is the independence number divided by (1 plus the monthly rate) raised to that many months. At a zero return the coast sum equals the independence number, because nothing grows while you wait. With a 7 percent nominal return compounded monthly and a 20-year gap, the coast sum for a 1,000,000 target is about 247,602. You would need about that much today if you planned to add nothing more and to let the rate you typed do the rest. If the age you stop adding is not above your age now, the gap in years is zero and the coast sum is the full independence number.

What stays outside

  • Inflation and taxes are outside. A nominal return can look fine while prices rise.
  • The return does not change, and contributions do not skip a month.
  • Four percent is a research tradition, not a promise about your sequence of returns.
  • Dividend income on today's portfolio is not the withdrawal, and it does not close the gap by itself.
  • A zero contribution and a zero return never close a gap.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate the FIRE number?

Divide annual spending by the withdrawal rate. Spending of 40000 divided by 0.04 is 1,000,000, which is 25 times one year of spending. The 4 percent figure is a rule of thumb from a historical research tradition, not a guarantee that future returns will support it. A higher rate makes the target smaller. A lower rate makes it larger. The spending you type is before tax. The form does not know your brackets or which costs are essential.

Is dividend income the same as the withdrawal rate?

No. Dividend income is today's portfolio times the yield you type. A portfolio of 250000 at 3 percent produces 7500 a year. The withdrawal rate is the fraction you assume you take from the full independence number. Four percent of 1,000,000 is 40000. The 7500 does not cover that spending, and it is not subtracted from the gap. A yield on a small portfolio and a withdrawal rate on the target are different lines.

What happens if I save nothing and the return is zero?

A gap is never closed. With no new contribution and no growth, the portfolio stays where it is, so it cannot catch an independence number that sits above it. The page says the contributions and the return do not reach the number. If the portfolio is already at or above the number, the gap is zero and the time is zero, even when the return is zero. A positive return with no contributions can still close a gap.

What is the coast sum on this page?

It is the amount that grows to the independence number with no new contributions across the age gap you type. That gap is the age you would stop adding money, minus your age now. The sum is the independence number divided by the monthly growth over those months, using the same monthly rate as the time estimate. At 7 percent compounded monthly, a 20-year gap, and a 1,000,000 target, the coast sum is about 247,602.

Does the FIRE calculator need a brokerage account?

No. The independence number, the gap, the dividend line, and the coast sum are calculated from the fields you fill in. A broker is only a place to hold a portfolio if you choose to invest. Interactive Brokers is one option people use across countries. Commissions and market access change, and this page does not quote them. Four percent is not a guarantee, and the return you typed is an assumption. Opening an account does not change the arithmetic.
