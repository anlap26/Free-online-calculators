Inflation calculator

One or two inflation rates you type, a future cost, what today's cash still buys, and a real return if you also type a nominal one.

This is arithmetic for a plan. It is not a forecast and not investment, tax, or legal advice.

Future cost is the amount times (1 plus the inflation rate) to the power of the years. Purchasing power of today's cash is the amount divided by that same power. Take 100 at 3 percent for 10 years. The same basket costs about 134.39 later, and 100 held as cash still buys about 74.41 of today's goods. One figure runs the rate forward. The other runs it backward on money you already have. They are not two opinions. A rate of zero leaves both figures equal to the amount you typed, which is a useful check that the form is doing what you think. The rate is constant for the whole span. A decade that will not look like one rate needs more than one run.

The real return, when you type a nominal one

Real return is (1 plus the nominal return) divided by (1 plus inflation), minus 1. A nominal 5 percent beside 3 percent inflation is 1.05 divided by 1.03, minus 1, about 1.94 percent a year. Leave the nominal field blank and the real return stays blank. The page will not invent a market return to fill the gap. If the nominal rate equals inflation, the real return is zero: you kept pace with the price rise and no more. This page does not grow a balance with contributions. That job belongs to the compound interest calculator, which wants a nominal rate and can take a monthly sum. Mixing the two rates inside one box hides which assumption moved.

Two currencies are two rates you type

The form has two names and two inflation rates. Both paths use the same amount, the same years, and the same optional nominal return. Only the inflation rate differs, so you can see two future costs and two real returns side by side. The names are labels for the lines. They do not convert one currency into the other, and they do not fetch a policy rate. Typing USD and EUR does not apply an exchange rate. The gap a bank hides between its rate and the interbank rate is the currency spread calculator, and you have to type both of those rates yourself. Numbers already filled in so the form is not empty are illustrations, not an official forecast. This page does not reprint a historical consumer-price series, and it will not pretend a default is this year's official figure.

A wallet and a price rise are different problems

  • The inflation rate does not change during the years you typed.
  • There is no tax, no fee, and no wage growth in the formula.
  • A blank nominal return means no real-return line, not a real return of zero.
  • City price indexes are a different page. They are not this power.
  • A device that holds coins does not change how fast a currency buys less.

Comparing what a salary buys in two cities is the cost of living calculator. Those indexes are a separate editorial table, not an inflation path. A portfolio target that ignores prices is the FIRE calculator. Protecting savings from custodial risk is a different problem from inflation. Ledger and Trezor are hardware wallets: coins kept on the device are not sitting on an exchange. That can matter if a company fails or locks an account. It does nothing to the rate at which a currency buys less. Device prices and the assets each wallet supports change. The Ledger and Trezor guide says what to check. It does not claim either device beats inflation. This page does the arithmetic whether or not you buy one.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate future cost from an inflation rate?

Multiply the amount by 1 plus the inflation rate, raised to the number of years. At 3 percent for 10 years, 100 becomes about 134.39. That is the later cost of the same basket under a rate that never changes. It is not an official forecast, and the numbers filled in so the form is not empty are illustrations. This page does not reprint a historical price index. If you expect the rate to change, run the years in pieces.

What is purchasing power of today's cash?

It is the amount divided by 1 plus inflation, raised to the years. The same 100 at 3 percent for 10 years still buys about 74.41 of today's goods. Future cost runs the rate forward on a basket. Purchasing power runs it backward on cash you hold now. A rate of zero leaves both figures equal to the amount. The two results are one formula read in opposite directions, not two separate predictions about policy or wages.

How do you turn a nominal return into a real return?

Divide 1 plus the nominal return by 1 plus inflation, then subtract 1. A nominal 5 percent beside 3 percent inflation is about 1.94 percent a year. If the two rates match, the real return is zero. Leave the nominal field blank and the real return stays blank. The page will not invent a market return. This formula does not grow a balance with monthly contributions. That is the compound interest calculator.

Do the two currency boxes convert money from one to the other?

No. Each name is a label, and each inflation rate is a number you type. Both paths share the amount, the years, and the optional nominal return. They do not apply an exchange rate, and typing a currency code does not fetch a central-bank figure. The gap between a bank rate and the interbank rate is a different calculator, and there you also type both rates. Defaults on this page are illustrations so the form is not empty.

Does a hardware wallet protect savings from inflation?

No. Inflation is a currency buying less over time. A hardware wallet is about custody: keeping coins off an exchange so they are not in a company's hands. Ledger and Trezor can help with that second problem if you use them as intended. They do not change the inflation rate you typed. Device prices and supported assets change. The calculator runs either way, and the guide compares what to check on each device.
