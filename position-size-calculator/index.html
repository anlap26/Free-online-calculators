Position size calculator

Cash at risk and the distance from entry to stop set the number of units, for a long or a short.

This is arithmetic for a plan. It is not a forecast and not investment, tax, or legal advice.

Size comes from the stop, not from an account offer

Units follow the cash you are willing to lose and the distance to the stop. Bybit is an exchange and Interactive Brokers is a broker. Either may be irrelevant to the market you trade. This is not a signal to take the trade. Fees are outside this result, and fee schedules change. The page works without signing up.

Cash at risk is the account times the risk percent. An account of 10000 and a risk of 1 percent put 100 in that line. For a long, the distance is the entry minus the stop, and it must be positive. Units are the cash at risk divided by that distance. Entry 50 and stop 48 give a distance of 2, so the units are 100 divided by 2, which is 50. Position value is units times entry, here 2500. Reward, if you type a target of 56, is 6 per unit times 50 units, which is 300. The R multiple is the reward per unit divided by the distance, 6 divided by 2, which is 3. Leave the target blank and both the reward and the R multiple stay blank. The page will not invent a profit target for you.

A small stop can still be a large position

The 2500 position is 25 percent of the 10000 account, while the cash at risk is only 100, which is 1 percent. That is leverage-like exposure. The loss if the stop fills at the stop price is the small number. The amount of the account tied up in the trade is the large one. A gap through the stop, a halt, or a fill worse than the stop can lose more than the 100. The formula assumes the stop fills at the price you typed. It does not model a gap. Fees are not included. A round trip that looks like 3R before fees can be less once the percent on each fill is taken off. The trading fee calculator is where that percent goes. This page will not fold it in quietly.

The short, and a stop on the wrong side

A short uses stop minus entry for the distance, and entry minus the target for the reward per unit. Both distances have to point the right way. Take the same account and the same 1 percent risk, with entry 50, stop 52, and target 44. The distance is 2, the units are 50, the position value is 2500, the reward is 300, and the R multiple is 3. The arithmetic mirrors the long. If the stop is on the wrong side, the tool errors. A long whose stop sits above the entry has no positive distance. A short whose stop sits below the entry has none either. A target on the losing side of the entry does not error. It produces a negative reward, which is the formula saying the target is not a profit. Read that minus sign before you treat the row as a plan.

Questions people ask

How do you calculate position size from a stop loss?

Multiply the account by the risk percent to get the cash at risk. For a long, subtract the stop from the entry. That distance must be positive. Divide the cash at risk by the distance to get the units. An account of 10000, risk of 1 percent, entry 50, and stop 48 give 100 of cash at risk, a distance of 2, and 50 units. Position value is 2500. The formula assumes the stop fills at the stop.

What is the R multiple in this calculator?

It is the reward per unit divided by the distance from entry to stop. With entry 50, stop 48, and target 56, the reward per unit is 6 and the distance is 2, so the multiple is 3. On 50 units the cash reward is 300. Leave the target blank and both the reward and the multiple stay blank. A target on the losing side of the entry does not error. It comes back negative.

How does a short position change the formula?

The distance is the stop minus the entry, and it still has to be positive, so the stop sits above the entry. The reward per unit is the entry minus the target. Entry 50, stop 52, and target 44, on the same 10000 account at 1 percent risk, again give 50 units, a position value of 2500, a reward of 300, and a multiple of 3. If the stop is on the wrong side, the tool errors.

Why can a 1 percent risk still use a large share of the account?

Cash at risk is the loss if the stop fills at the stop price. Position value is units times the entry. In the example the cash at risk is 100, which is 1 percent of 10000, while the position is 2500, which is 25 percent of the account. A tight stop produces a large size. That is leverage-like exposure. A gap through the stop can lose more than the cash at risk.

Does position size include trading fees?

No. Units come only from the cash at risk and the distance to the stop. A fee on the way in and on the way out can shrink the reward, and this page does not subtract it. Use the trading fee calculator for the percent and the slippage, and keep the two results apart. The figure here is also not trading advice and not a signal to open the trade. An exchange or broker account is optional.
